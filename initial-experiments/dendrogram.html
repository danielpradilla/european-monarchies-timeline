Seven reigns from 1519, forced into a tree

I tried grouping crowns the dynastic way: one root, then the ruling house, then the crown, then the reign. The seven records below come from the project dataset and were picked for this illustration. They are a sample of who reigned in 1519, not the full list of crowns or rulers.

Where the tree splits one identity

  • Charles V is two leaves. reign-charles-v-hre and reign-charles-v-spain share person_id charles-v, but the hierarchy only knows they sit under the same house.
  • Spain is two branches. From 1516 Joanna (Trastámara) and Charles (Habsburg) held the Spanish crowns jointly, so the dataset keeps two reigns on polity_id spain and the tree files them under different houses. Spain here is the dataset's composite dynastic phase; Castile and Aragon keep their own records.
  • Joanna is three leaves, for Castile, Aragon and Spain. The record notes say she kept the royal title while Charles governed. They also call him her son, and this tree has no line for that.

What a tree cannot draw

  • Parents and marriages. A person has two parents, often from two houses, so Charles belongs under Habsburg and next to his Trastámara mother at once.
  • Elections. Charles became King of the Romans by election in 1519. An elective crown can pass between families, and the tree would have to move the whole branch.
  • Unions. One ruler on several crowns, two rulers on one crown, and unions that are interrupted or end while both states carry on all need edges across branches. Add them and the tree becomes a network.

How to read the marks

  • From the dataset: names, IDs, house and crown assignments, years and citations.
  • Layout choices: branch order, the even row spacing and the fixed column for each level. Branch length means nothing; it does not encode time, descent or power.
  • Houses are groupings of reign records. They are not drawn as ancestors of the crowns, and the order of houses is not a ranking.
