Designing the timeline

Two thousand years of European crowns, on one timeline

I wanted to see which monarchies existed at the same time. That simple question turned into a problem about names, borders, family trees and the misleading confidence of a beautiful diagram.

Most histories of monarchy arrive one country at a time. You learn the Tudors, then perhaps the Bourbons, the Habsburgs or the Romanovs. Each line makes sense on its own. What disappears is simultaneity.

Henry VIII and Francis I were near-contemporaries. Charles V ruled Spain while also becoming Holy Roman emperor. James VI of Scotland could become James I of England without Scotland and England becoming the same country. Those are easy facts to recite and surprisingly hard facts to hold in your head at once.

The project began as six coloured bars. Rome, Byzantium, England, France, the Holy Roman Empire and Castile. It proved that a shared time axis was useful, but little else. Every bar looked independent. There was no way to show that a single person held several crowns, that two kingdoms shared a monarch, or that one political phase ended by becoming part of another.

The temptation of the river

A Sankey diagram looked like the obvious next step. Monarchies could become streams. They could split, curve and merge. The result would resemble one of those long historical charts where civilizations flow down the page. John B. Sparks's 1931 Histomap is still compelling for exactly that reason.

It would also lie.

A Sankey needs a quantity. A thick line means more energy, money, material or people passed through it. What would the thickness of France mean here? Territory? Population? Revenue? Military power? Those measures exist for some periods and collapse into guesswork for others. If line width carried no value, the chart would borrow the authority of a quantitative form without doing the accounting.

So every monarchy in this timeline has the same height. Duration is the only continuous measurement. The visual can say that the Byzantine Empire lasted longer than the Kingdom of Italy. It does not pretend that Byzantium was six times more powerful.

A family tree was no better

The second idea was a dendrogram. Monarchies are dynastic, after all. Put the houses in a tree and let the branches explain the crowns.

That works until the first marriage, election, restoration or disputed succession. A person has two parents. A royal house has cadet branches. An elective crown can move between families. The same monarch can hold several titles, and a personal union can end without either state ending. Europe does not give you a tree. It gives you a graph with a habit of tying itself in knots.

The final design keeps a timeline as its spine. Political phases are bars. Houses are narrower bands inside them. Selected reigns are dots with short duration lines. Typed connectors do the work that a family tree or Sankey would otherwise blur together.

Five dates that explain the chart

843: an empire becomes a set of arguments

The Treaty of Verdun divided the Carolingian Empire among the sons of Louis the Pious. It did not manufacture France and Germany in their modern form. It did produce western, middle and eastern political lines that later rulers kept reusing.

On the chart, Francia ends in 843. A partition connector points toward France and toward the precursor story of the Holy Roman Empire. The line is an interpretation, so the detail panel says exactly what it means.

Open the timeline at 843

1066: a house changes, a kingdom does not

William's conquest of England is a clean example of why house and polity need separate records. The Kingdom of England continues. The ruling house changes from Wessex to Normandy. A monarch dot sits on the new band, but the longer English phase bar does not restart.

Open the timeline at 1066

1519: Charles V breaks the one-person, one-country model

This is the date I keep coming back to. Charles inherited the Spanish crowns in 1516 and was elected emperor in 1519. He did not fuse Spain and the Holy Roman Empire into one ordinary state. He was one person with several reigns.

The data therefore has one Charles V and separate reign records. Move the year line to 1519 and his name appears in more than one lane. That is a small database decision with a large visual payoff.

Open the timeline at 1519

1707: sharing a monarch is not the same as sharing a state

James VI of Scotland inherited the English crown in 1603. The two kingdoms shared a sovereign for more than a century while keeping separate parliaments. In 1707 the Acts of Union created Great Britain.

Those two relationships need different marks. The first is a dashed personal-union connector. The second is a state-union connector that ends the English and Scottish lanes and begins the Great Britain lane.

Open the timeline at 1707

1918: several long lines stop together

The end of the First World War is where the continental comparison earns its space. The German and Austro-Hungarian monarchies end in the same narrow slice of time. The Russian monarchy had fallen the year before. The Ottoman sultanate survives a little longer, until 1922.

A national timeline shows one collapse. The shared axis shows a political system coming apart across the continent.

Modern countries make poor containers for medieval history

There is no honest way to draw this using today's countries as permanent rows. "Germany" before 1871 would swallow the Holy Roman Empire, Prussia, Austria, Bohemia and dozens of states that did not think of themselves as administrative pieces of a future nation. "Italy" would do the same to Savoy, Sardinia, Sicily, the Papal States and the northern republics.

The timeline uses historical polities instead. Some are composite. Some overlap. England, Great Britain and the United Kingdom get separate lanes because their constitutional identities differ. Spain overlaps Castile and Aragon during the dynastic period because a shared monarchy did not immediately erase either crown.

This makes the chart messier than a classroom poster. Good. The mess is part of the subject.

The dates need footnotes

The Holy Roman Empire can begin in 800 with Charlemagne or in 962 with Otto I. France can begin in 843 with West Francia or in 987 with Hugh Capet. The Roman Empire did not experience 395 as a modern corporate split.

I chose dates that make the cross-European comparison legible, then recorded the alternatives in notes and events. Charlemagne's coronation remains visible even though the continuous Holy Roman lane begins with Otto. France begins with West Francia, but the Capetian band starts later. The chart does not eliminate the disagreement. It gives the disagreement somewhere to live.

A chart for browsing, not a final answer

The current dataset has forty-five polity lanes, 187 selected reigns and thirty-six typed relationships. It includes every surviving European monarchy and a broad set of former kingdoms and empires. It still leaves things out. The German and Italian states alone could fill another project. So could royal genealogy, colonial titles or the elected kings of the Commonwealth.

The overview is meant to produce questions. Why does this lane stop? Why does that house appear in three countries? Who else was ruling in 1519? Each selected mark opens a short explanation and its references. The methodology page records the scope and the known compromises.

I started by wanting one big timeline. What I ended up needing was a timeline that admits where the lines are uncertain.
